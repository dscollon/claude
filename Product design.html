01

Module 01 · 2–3 weeks

Understanding Users & Problems

Before designing anything, learn to ask: who is this for, and what frustration are they currently living with?

What you'll learn

  • The difference between a user need and a product feature
  • How to observe and interview potential customers to surface real problems
  • Jobs-to-be-Done theory — people don't buy products, they 'hire' them for a job
  • How to map user journeys to find pain points worth solving

📚 Books

+
The Design of Everyday Things
Don Norman · 2013 revised edition

Norman's central argument is simple but profound: when a product is hard to use, the fault lies with the design, not the user. He builds this case through a set of core concepts that every designer needs to understand.

Affordances are the possible actions an object suggests — a door handle affords pulling, a flat plate affords pushing. When affordances are well designed, you know what to do without thinking. Signifiers are the signals that communicate where and how to act — a label saying "push here," a visible slot, a raised button. Good design makes signifiers obvious; bad design hides them or sends the wrong signals entirely.

Feedback is what happens after you take an action — a click, a sound, a visual change. Without feedback, users feel like they're operating in a void. Mental models are the internal pictures people form about how something works. Great design matches the product's actual behaviour to the user's mental model. When these diverge — when the product behaves differently from what the user expects — confusion and frustration follow.

Norman also introduces the concept of human error — and argues that most "user errors" are actually design errors. If people consistently misuse something the same way, that's the design's fault, not theirs. He distinguishes between slips (doing the right thing wrong, like pressing the wrong button) and mistakes (doing the wrong thing entirely, based on a flawed mental model). Good design reduces both.

The book closes with a discussion of design process: the importance of iteration, observation, and testing with real users rather than relying on assumptions about how people will behave.

Why it matters for you: Reading this changes how you look at every object around you. You'll start seeing why certain 3D-printed items feel satisfying and others get put in a drawer.

+
When Coffee and Kale Compete
Alan Klement · 2018 (free online)

Klement's book is built around a single insight: people don't buy products — they hire them to make progress in some part of their life. A person doesn't buy a milkshake because they love milkshakes; they hire it to get through a boring morning commute without feeling hungry or making a mess. Understanding that job — and all the competing options they might hire instead — is what leads to products that genuinely resonate.

The Jobs-to-be-Done framework has two dimensions. Functional jobs are the practical tasks people are trying to accomplish ("I need to store my phone somewhere visible while I cook"). Emotional jobs are how people want to feel while accomplishing that task ("I want to feel organised and in control of my kitchen"). Products that address both dimensions outperform those that address only one.

Klement also introduces the idea of competing against non-consumption — your real competition isn't always another product. Sometimes it's the customer's current workaround, or simply doing nothing. If someone's current solution to a problem is an elastic band around their phone, a £30 printed stand needs to be dramatically better to justify switching.

The book gives practical techniques for uncovering jobs: timeline interviews (asking customers to walk you through exactly what happened before, during, and after they used a product), and struggle mapping (identifying the moments of friction that made them look for a solution in the first place). These are the conversations that reveal what people actually value — which is often quite different from what they say they want.

Why it matters for you: Instead of thinking "I'll print a phone stand," you think: "What job is someone hiring a phone stand to do?" Shifts guessing to purposeful design. Available free online.

🛠 Practical Exercise

Pick one item you currently sell. Find 3 people who own or might want it. Ask them: "Tell me about the last time you needed something like this — what were you trying to do?" Don't show them your product. Just listen. Write down every frustration and goal they mention.

02

Module 02 · 2–3 weeks

Market Research & Opportunity Spotting

Learn to look at what's already selling, find the gaps, and validate demand before spending a minute in your slicer.

What you'll learn

  • How to read Etsy and Amazon reviews as a research tool (the "review mining" technique)
  • How to use search trends and keyword tools to gauge real demand
  • How to spot underserved niches by looking at complaints in existing products
  • The difference between a market gap and a market that simply doesn't exist

📚 Books

+
Obviously Awesome
April Dunford · 2019

Positioning is the act of deliberately defining how your product is the best solution for a specific type of customer with a specific problem. Dunford argues that most products fail not because they're bad, but because they're poorly positioned — the right customers can't identify themselves in the description, or the product is being compared to the wrong competitors.

Her process starts with competitive alternatives — not who you think your competitors are, but what a customer would actually do if your product didn't exist. For a premium 3D-printed desk organiser, the alternative might not be another Etsy seller; it might be a cheap plastic tray from a supermarket, or just leaving the desk messy. Understanding that alternative is the baseline everything else is measured against.

Next comes identifying your unique attributes — the specific ways your product is genuinely different from that alternative. Not better in every way, but meaningfully better in at least one dimension that matters. Then, crucially, you identify which customers care most about that specific difference. Not all customers value the same things; the goal is to find the ones for whom your differentiator is a real priority.

From there, Dunford works through market category (what kind of product are you, and what does that category cue in the buyer's mind?) and relevant trends (what's happening in the world that makes your product more timely and relevant right now?). The book ends with a practical positioning template that can be adapted directly to product listings, descriptions, and marketing copy.

Why it matters for you: Helps you stop competing on price and start carving out a niche where you're the obvious answer. Directly applicable to how you describe and position products in your Etsy shop.

+
Competing Against Luck
Clayton Christensen et al. · 2016

Christensen's central argument is that innovation has been treated as inherently unpredictable — a matter of luck — when in fact it can be made systematic. The key is shifting focus from customer demographics and product attributes to the job the customer is trying to get done. Companies that understand the job deeply build products that customers pull into their lives rather than ones that need to be pushed through marketing.

The book introduces the idea that jobs have functional, emotional, and social dimensions — and that overlooking any of them leads to incomplete products. A small safe bought for a home isn't just hired to protect valuables (functional); it's also hired to give the owner peace of mind (emotional) and to signal that they're a responsible adult (social). Products that address only the functional dimension leave the emotional and social jobs unfulfilled, making them vulnerable to displacement.

Christensen makes a crucial distinction between the circumstances of the job and the demographic of the customer. Two people with identical demographics can hire a product for completely different jobs — and two people with very different demographics can share exactly the same job. This is why demographic-based market research so often misleads: it groups customers by who they are rather than by what they're trying to accomplish.

The book's most practical chapter concerns data and the job — why customer data often obscures jobs rather than revealing them, and how to design research that surfaces the real motivations behind purchases. It argues strongly for qualitative, narrative research (talking to customers about their lives) over quantitative surveys, which tend to confirm assumptions rather than challenge them.

Why it matters for you: Ideal companion to Module 1. Helps you see how to systematically look for underserved jobs in your product category.

Module 03 · 2–3 weeks

Design Principles & Form

Learn the timeless principles behind why some products feel well-made and others feel cheap — and how to apply them to physical objects.

What you'll learn

  • Core visual principles: proportion, balance, hierarchy, contrast, and rhythm
  • Why simplicity increases perceived value
  • How material, texture, and finish affect how people feel about a product
  • The psychology of aesthetics — why certain forms feel satisfying

📚 Books

+
The Laws of Simplicity
John Maeda · 2006

Maeda's book is itself a demonstration of its own argument — it's short, precise, and wastes nothing. The ten laws are principles for thinking about reduction and simplicity, illustrated with examples from product design, technology, and art.

The first and most important law is Reduce: the simplest way to achieve simplicity is through thoughtful reduction — removing features, steps, or visual elements until you reach the point where removing anything more would damage the core function. This is harder than it sounds, because addition feels productive and reduction feels risky. The second law, Organise, argues that organising a system well can make complexity feel simpler without removing anything — grouping related things, establishing clear hierarchies, and creating visual order all reduce cognitive load.

Time is the third law: anything that saves time feels simpler, because time spent waiting or struggling is experienced as complexity. Learn argues that knowledge removes the perception of complexity — once you understand something, it no longer feels hard. This has implications for product instructions, packaging, and anything that onboards a new user.

Maeda also articulates a useful paradox: simplicity and complexity need each other. A product that is too simple offers nothing; context and contrast make simplicity feel meaningful rather than empty. The goal is not minimalism for its own sake, but the precise amount of complexity needed — and no more. His final law sums it up: "Simplicity is about subtracting the obvious and adding the meaningful."

Why it matters for you: Directly applicable to 3D printing. The tendency in CAD is to add — features, details, complexity. This book teaches you when and how to take away instead.

+
100 Things Every Designer Needs to Know About People
Susan Weinschenk · 2011

Weinschenk distils a large body of psychology and neuroscience research into 100 discrete, actionable insights for designers. Each one is short — a page or two — and directly connected to a design implication. The book is less a narrative and more a reference, but read front to back it builds a coherent picture of the gap between how designers assume people behave and how they actually do.

On vision and perception: people don't see the world as it actually is — they see a constructed interpretation based on expectations, contrast, and context. Colour perception changes dramatically depending on surrounding colours. People's eyes are drawn to faces before almost anything else. Objects at the edges of a composition get less attention than those near the centre or along natural reading paths. These insights have immediate implications for product photography and shop presentation.

On attention and memory: people can only hold about four chunks of information in working memory at once. Anything that asks for more than that creates cognitive overload and a sense of effort. Chunking — grouping related information — reduces this burden. People remember stories, emotions, and beginnings and endings far better than they remember details in the middle of an experience.

On motivation and decision-making: people are strongly motivated by progress — even the illusion of progress (like a loyalty card with two stamps already on it) increases follow-through. They overweight losses relative to equivalent gains (loss aversion). They are heavily influenced by what they believe others are doing (social proof). And they make most decisions intuitively and emotionally, then rationalise them afterwards — which means that how a product feels in the hand, or how a listing looks at a glance, matters more than the logical arguments made in the description.

Why it matters for you: Helps you understand why certain colours, shapes, and forms attract attention or signal quality. Immediately useful for both product design and product photography.

Module 04 · 2 weeks

Ideation & Concept Development

Move from a vague idea to a concrete concept worth printing — using structured techniques to generate, filter, and develop ideas.

What you'll learn

  • How to use "How Might We" questions to reframe problems as opportunities
  • Brainstorming and SCAMPER techniques for physical products
  • How to evaluate ideas using desirability, feasibility, and viability
  • Creating rough concept sketches to compare ideas before you model anything

📚 Books

+
Creative Confidence
Tom & David Kelley · 2013

The Kelley brothers' core thesis is that creative confidence — the belief that you can and will come up with good ideas — is something most people lose in childhood and can consciously rebuild as adults. The experience of being told your drawing isn't good, or that your idea is impractical, creates what they call a "creativity scar" that causes people to self-censor before they even begin. The antidote is structured practice and small wins.

A central technique is prototyping early and often — building rough, imperfect versions of ideas specifically to get feedback and learn, not to produce final outputs. The goal of an early prototype isn't to be right; it's to be fast and cheap enough that being wrong costs nothing. This shifts the emotional stakes of creating from "I need this to be good" to "I'm just exploring." Applied to 3D printing, this means deliberately printing quick, low-quality test versions rather than spending hours perfecting a model before anyone has seen it.

The book introduces the concept of design thinking as a human-centred process with five stages: empathise (understand the person you're designing for), define (frame the specific problem worth solving), ideate (generate many possible solutions without judgment), prototype (build quickly and cheaply), and test (get feedback from real people). These stages aren't strictly linear — you move back and forth between them as you learn.

On ideation specifically, the Kelleys advocate for separating the generation phase from the evaluation phase. During brainstorming, judgment is suspended entirely — the goal is quantity, not quality, because quantity creates the raw material from which good ideas are selected. Premature criticism (including self-criticism) kills generative momentum. Only after a large pool of ideas exists should filtering begin.

Why it matters for you: Gives you concrete tools to deliberately generate more and better ideas, and to feel confident experimenting — rather than waiting for inspiration.

+
Hooked: How to Build Habit-Forming Products
Nir Eyal · 2014

Eyal's Hook Model describes the mechanism by which products build habits — and understanding it helps designers create things people return to, rather than things they use once and forget. The model has four stages that cycle continuously, each pass deepening the user's attachment.

The first stage is the trigger — the prompt that initiates the behaviour. External triggers are things in the environment: a notification, an advertisement, a friend's recommendation, seeing the product on a desk. Internal triggers are emotions and states that become associated with using the product — boredom, anxiety, a desire to feel organised. The most durable habits are driven by internal triggers, but products almost always start with external ones.

The second stage is the action — the simplest behaviour done in anticipation of a reward. Eyal draws on BJ Fogg's behavioural model here: behaviour happens when motivation, ability, and a trigger converge at the same moment. Reducing friction (making the action easier) is often more effective than trying to increase motivation. For physical products, this might mean making something easier to pick up, store visibly, or incorporate into an existing routine.

The third stage is the variable reward — and the "variable" element is key. Predictable rewards lose their power quickly; variable rewards (where you don't quite know what you'll get) maintain engagement far longer. Eyal identifies three reward types: rewards of the tribe (social connection and approval), rewards of the hunt (resources and information), and rewards of the self (intrinsic satisfaction, mastery, completion). The final stage is investment — the user puts something into the product (data, effort, time, social capital) that makes it more valuable to them personally and increases the likelihood they'll return.

Why it matters for you: Helps you design products people keep using, come back to, and recommend. Useful for thinking about repeat purchases and product ecosystems.

Module 05 · 2–3 weeks

Rapid Prototyping & Iteration

You already have the best rapid prototyping tool available — your printer. This module teaches you how to structure your test cycles to learn faster and waste less.

What you'll learn

  • The Build-Measure-Learn loop from Lean Startup methodology
  • How to define what you're testing before you hit print
  • The difference between concept tests, usability tests, and market tests
  • How to get useful feedback from potential buyers without giving the product away

📚 Books

+
The Lean Startup
Eric Ries · 2011

Ries wrote The Lean Startup in response to a pattern he observed repeatedly: talented teams building products with great care and technical skill — only to launch them and discover that nobody wanted them. The problem wasn't execution; it was that they had been executing against the wrong assumptions. The solution is to treat those assumptions as hypotheses and test them as cheaply and quickly as possible before committing to full production.

The core engine of the book is the Build-Measure-Learn loop. Every cycle starts with identifying your most important untested assumption — the thing that, if wrong, would cause the whole idea to fail. You then build the smallest possible thing that tests that assumption (the Minimum Viable Product, or MVP), measure what actually happens when real people encounter it, and use what you learn to decide whether to persevere with the current direction or pivot to a new one.

The MVP is often misunderstood as a rough version of the final product. Ries is clear that it's something different: it's the fastest, cheapest thing that tests a specific hypothesis. Sometimes it's a landing page with no product behind it, just to see if people click "buy." Sometimes it's a manual service delivered by hand before building any automation. For a 3D printing seller, an MVP might be a photo of a product concept posted in a relevant Facebook group before printing a single copy — just to see if people ask where to buy it.

The book also introduces innovation accounting — the idea that startups (and any small creative business) need different metrics than established companies. Rather than measuring revenue (which is a lagging indicator), you measure the assumptions underlying future revenue: Are people finding the product? Are they returning? Are they telling others? Tracking these leading indicators tells you whether you're making real progress or just busy.

Why it matters for you: The core principle maps perfectly to 3D printing: print the minimum version that tests your key assumption, get feedback, improve. Stops you building full production runs of things nobody wants.

+
Prototyping and Modelmaking for Product Design
Bjarki Hallgrimsson · 2012

Hallgrimsson's book treats prototyping not as a single activity but as a family of related activities, each serving a different purpose at a different stage of the design process. Understanding which type of prototype to make — and why — is as important as knowing how to make it.

Sketch models are the earliest and roughest form — made quickly from foam, cardboard, or tape to explore the size, proportion, and feel of an idea in three dimensions. Their purpose is not to look good but to get an idea out of your head and into the physical world, where it can be evaluated honestly. A sketch model that takes 20 minutes to make can answer questions about ergonomics and scale that hours of CAD cannot.

Appearance models (sometimes called looks-like prototypes) focus on the aesthetic qualities of a product — surface finish, colour, material feel — without necessarily functioning. They're used to evaluate whether something looks right and to get reactions from potential customers before committing to production tooling. Functional prototypes (works-like prototypes) test the mechanical or structural behaviour of a design — whether it clicks, holds weight, fits together, or moves correctly — without necessarily looking finished.

The book makes a strong argument for prototyping in parallel rather than in sequence — building multiple rough concepts simultaneously rather than perfecting one at a time. This prevents premature attachment to a single idea and keeps options open longer. It also covers user testing with prototypes: how to present them to people, what questions to ask, and how to interpret the feedback you get — including how to distinguish between "I wouldn't buy this" feedback (about the concept) and "this part doesn't work well" feedback (about the execution).

Why it matters for you: The only book on this list written specifically about physical prototyping. Highly practical for someone with 3D printing capability.

Module 06 · 2 weeks

Pricing, Value & Positioning

Understand the psychology behind perceived value — and learn to design and present products so that your price feels entirely reasonable.

What you'll learn

  • Why cost-plus pricing undervalues most handmade products
  • Value-based pricing — pricing to the customer's perceived benefit, not your material cost
  • How design quality, packaging, photography, and copy all signal value
  • How to position a product so the right buyer self-selects

📚 Books

+
Obviously Awesome (revisited)
April Dunford · 2019

When revisited through the lens of pricing and value communication, Dunford's book reveals a layer that's easy to miss on first reading: positioning doesn't just determine who buys your product — it determines how much they're willing to pay for it.

The concept of competitive alternatives is especially relevant here. Your price is always evaluated relative to the alternative a customer would use if your product didn't exist. If the alternative is a cheap mass-produced version of what you make, your positioning needs to explicitly frame why yours is worth more — through context, story, and specificity. If the alternative is a completely different category of solution (hiring someone, doing without, making their own), you have more pricing freedom because the comparison is harder to make directly.

Dunford's chapter on market categories is the most useful for pricing. The category you position yourself in sets a price expectation before the customer has even looked at your product. A "desk tidy" competes with £5 options from supermarkets; a "handcrafted walnut desk organiser" operates in a different category with different price expectations. Small changes in framing — the words you use, the photos you take, the context you create — can move a product between categories entirely, with significant pricing implications.

The book also addresses the risk of under-positioning — failing to stake out a clear position and trying to appeal to everyone. Products positioned for everyone tend to be chosen by nobody, because they don't speak clearly to anyone's specific situation. Narrowing your positioning feels risky but almost always increases both conversion and the price customers are willing to accept.

Why it matters for you: Re-read with a focus on the chapters about value and competitive alternatives. You'll see your listings quite differently.

+
Building a StoryBrand
Donald Miller · 2017

Miller's central claim is that most businesses fail to communicate clearly not because their products are bad, but because they position themselves as the hero of the story rather than the guide. Customers don't want to buy from heroes — they want to be the hero themselves, with your product as the tool that helps them succeed. Clarifying this relationship transforms how you describe what you sell.

The StoryBrand framework has seven elements, drawn from the structure of narrative that appears in stories across cultures. The character is the customer — not you, not your product. The problem is what the customer is struggling with (which exists at three levels: the external problem they can articulate, the internal frustration they feel because of it, and the philosophical sense that things shouldn't be this way). The guide is you — showing up with empathy (you understand their problem) and authority (you've solved it before).

The guide then presents a plan — a simple, clear set of steps that remove the customer's uncertainty about how to proceed. This is followed by a call to action — asking the customer to do something specific, directly. Miller is emphatic that vague calls to action ("check us out," "learn more") perform far worse than specific ones ("buy now," "order yours today"). The story arc resolves in two directions: success (what the customer's life looks like after buying) and failure (what remains true if they don't act).

For Etsy sellers, the most immediately useful application is in product descriptions and shop copy. Most product descriptions describe the product ("this stand is made from PETG and measures 12cm wide"). The StoryBrand approach describes the customer's life ("keep your workbench clear and your tools where you can reach them"). The shift from product-centred to customer-centred language consistently increases both engagement and conversion.

Why it matters for you: Immediately applicable to product titles, descriptions, and your shop's About page. Helps your listings communicate value in seconds rather than paragraphs.

🛠 Practical Exercise

Take your best-selling product. Write a new description using the StoryBrand framework: (1) Who is the customer? (2) What problem do they have? (3) How does your product solve it? (4) What does their life look like after they buy it? Then A/B test the listing against your current one.

A Note on How to Use This

Don't wait until you've read everything before applying it. The best way to learn product design is to read a bit, make something, observe what happens, and come back to the reading with fresh questions.

You already have a significant advantage: you can print a prototype in hours. Most product designers would kill for that. Use the frameworks here to aim your printing at things more likely to resonate — and you'll find your hit rate improving noticeably.

Start with Module 1 and The Design of Everyday Things. Everything else flows from being able to see the world through a user's eyes.
